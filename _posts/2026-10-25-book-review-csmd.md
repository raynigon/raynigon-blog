# The Book

In his Book "Could Should Might Don't: How We Think About the Future" Nick Foster gives us four perspectives too look at the future(s).

## Could
The first one is the "could" perspective, where SciFi, wishes and Expo presentations live.
One good example for this "could" futurism is the new "flying" car by tesla. A car that no one asked for, no one needs and which drove the tesla stock down. Of course there are some people who will buy it, but this will likely never justify the development costs of this vehicle.

![Flying Car by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/flying-car.jpg)

Therefore this will likely be written of in a marketing budget and we will not speak again of it, in a few weeks/months.
But why is that? A flying car sounds like a dream come true for many SciFi fans. It sounds like a good, bright future and still, it does not feel right.
And this feeling is grounded in a simple statement "flying cars are impractical" (at least right now).
The legislative will not allow this kind of vehicle to be driven by a 17 year old (or whatever people can drive cars in your area). Using it will create a hazard for pedestrians and you probably end up on the edge of a bridge, when trying to overtake vertically.

Just to be honest, i want to have a flying car if possible, but i doubt our world will be adaptable to a greater amount of flying cars than perhabs a few thousands.

In his book, Nick Foster showcases that "could" futurism is always used to tell stories about a better, greater and advanced future where all/some problems are gone. I think this is a good way to look at the flying car:
Its a good marketing story, makes great pictures, but it will not likely be a real future, we will experience.

## Should
The second perspective is the "should" futurism, 
where a call to action is made. 
"We should to this, so that ..." is a sentence you will likely hear talking to a "should" futurist. People who live in this mindset are often an activists of some kind.

![Ice bear rescue by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/ice-bear-rescue.jpg)

This is no surprise since "should" futurism is usually driven by morals, ethics and urgency. The biggest "should" future topic in our age, seems to be climate change. "We should do something about the global temperatures rising!" is something many people are convinced of, because it fits their moral understanding, seems to be ethical correct and the topic is urgent.
Unfortunately we always seem to not be doing enough, or are we?
Since "should" futurism is driven by these high standards, there will never be an "enough". We do not know when we are finished. So every day, everyone  involved in this topic, needs to ask: What is the right thing to do?

This is all far away from cool technology ([and yes decarbonization of our athomsphere sounds cool, but...](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2025/may/17/swiss-firm-that-captures-carbon-from-air-to-cut-workforce-by-more-than-10)), advanced societies and a problem free world. In the best case, we have it as good as we have it now.

This type of futurism can also become problematic, if a mismatch in ethics, values or urgency occurs, some people might tend to be too idealistic or rigid. Conflicts can evolve, undermine the good cause and its future.
Nevertheless do we need these mindsets to drive initiatives in the world to ensure our survival.

## Might

The third and most complex perspective is the "might" one. It introduces the planners/strategist mindest and roots the future in science, data and math instead of dreaming or demanding. Might futurism deals with many possibilities which need to be weighted against each other. 

![Lunch Monk by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/lunch-monk.jpg)

Earlier in my career I worked for a company which developed optimization software for ground handling on airports. 
This context does not allow you to plan exact, you never know the exact order the planes will arrive. Sometimes a plane is delayed, sometimes they are too early and then there is quite a lot going on, before the plan can take off again. 
One lesson I took from this time is: "Do not decide until the last moment you need to". 
What it means, is that you need to prepare for all scenarios and decide in the exact moment before the first scenario becomes invalid (can not be used anymore).
In the ground handling context, this could lead to baggage belts waiting on an already loaded plane, because its not decided yet, which plane is the next one, but the moment the baggage belt takes longer to drive, than the plane to arrive, the driver is sent of to the most likeliest position.

This kind of futurism is hard mental work. Getting the data, preparing the scenarios and still missing many of them, can be exhausting. But this is still the most practical way to prepare for the future.

## Don't

The fourth and final perspective Foster introduces is "don't" futurism. This is the domain of the professional pessimist, the risk manager, and the institutional contrarian. While the other perspectives focus on what can or might happen, people in this mindset are focused on a single, vital question: What could go wrong?

![Dangerous Bridge by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/dangerous-bridge.jpg)

This mindset stands in direct contrast to the bright-eyed "could" futurists we started with. If a "could" futurist stands on a stage and excitedly announces, "Let's put AI chips in everyone's brains for instant telepathy!", the "don't" futurist is the one immediately raising their hand to talk about brain-hacking, the permanent loss of cognitive privacy, and absolute ethical nightmares.

We see this perspective in action all the time in the tech world. Think of data privacy advocates fighting against surveillance capitalism, AI safety researchers pleading for development pauses, or regulators blocking risky, untested technologies from hitting the public market. They are the ones looking at the bridge in the picture above and pointing out that it's just too dangerous to cross, no matter how cool the destination on the other side might be.

Because of their cautious nature, "don't" futurists are frequently dismissed as stubborn roadblocks to innovation. However, in the EU, this kind of thinking is really helpful and used in many applications (e.g. german product security law, where you must prove first, that the product is no harm, before selling it).
Foster also makes a compelling case for why we desperately need them. They provide the necessary friction to slow us down, ensuring that unchecked enthusiasm doesn't lead us into catastrophic and irreversible mistakes.

# Takeaway

These four perspectives help me a lot in my daily doing. Of course i can not do a strategic foresighting workshop, to get a better idea of what i should cook for dinner, i take different approaches on other topics in my life.
Using a "could" futurism to generate visions allows me to shine a little bit of light into the future i want to happen. With "should" futurism i can determine the environment and how it will influence the future. The "might" futurism, helps me to analyze plans, actions and strategies in detail and compare the in the different futures I expect. While the "Don't" futurism ensure I will not step over the line, because I take the warnings serious and evaluate them against the benefits of my decisions.

I hope this post gave you a good summary about whats in the book. If that sounds like your kind of thing, you should definitely check it out.
