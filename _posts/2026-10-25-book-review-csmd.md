# The Book

In his book "Could Should Might Don't: How We Think About the Future," Nick Foster gives us four perspectives to look at the future.

## Could
The first one is the "could" perspective, where sci-fi, wishes, and expo presentations live.
One good example of this "could" futurism is the new "flying" car by Tesla. It is a car that no one asked for, no one needs, and which drove the Tesla stock down. Of course, there are some people who will buy it, but this will likely never justify the development costs of the vehicle.

![Flying Car by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/flying-car.jpg)

Therefore, this will likely be written off as a marketing budget expense, and we will not speak of it again in a few weeks or months.
But why is that? A flying car sounds like a dream come true for many sci-fi fans. It sounds like a good, bright future, and still, it does not feel right.
This feeling is grounded in a simple statement: "flying cars are impractical" (at least right now).
Legislatures will not allow this kind of vehicle to be driven by a 17-year-old (or whatever the legal driving age is in your area). Using it would create a hazard for pedestrians, and you would probably end up on the edge of a bridge when trying to overtake vertically.

Just to be honest, I want to have a flying car if possible, but I doubt our world will be adaptable to a greater amount of flying cars than perhaps a few thousand.

In his book, Nick Foster showcases that "could" futurism is always used to tell stories about a better, greater, and advanced future where all or some problems are gone. I think this is a good way to look at the flying car:
It's a good marketing story and makes great pictures, but it will likely not be a real future we will experience.

## Should
The second perspective is "should" futurism, where a call to action is made. 
"We should do this, so that..." is a sentence you will likely hear when talking to a "should" futurist. People who live in this mindset are often activists of some kind.

![Ice bear rescue by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/ice-bear-rescue.jpg)

This is no surprise since "should" futurism is usually driven by morals, ethics, and urgency. The biggest "should" future topic in our age seems to be climate change. "We should do something about rising global temperatures!" is something many people are convinced of because it fits their moral understanding, seems ethically correct, and is an urgent topic.
Unfortunately, we always seem to not be doing enough—or are we?
Since "should" futurism is driven by these high standards, there will never be an "enough." We do not know when we are finished. So every day, everyone involved in this topic needs to ask: What is the right thing to do?

This is all far away from cool technology ([and yes, decarbonization of our atmosphere sounds cool, but...](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2025/may/17/swiss-firm-that-captures-carbon-from-air-to-cut-workforce-by-more-than-10)), advanced societies, and a problem-free world. In the best case, we have it as good as we have it now.

This type of futurism can also become problematic. If a mismatch in ethics, values, or urgency occurs, some people might tend to be too idealistic or rigid. Conflicts can evolve, undermining the good cause and its future.
Nevertheless, we need these mindsets to drive initiatives in the world to ensure our survival.

## Might

The third and most complex perspective is the "might" perspective. It introduces the planner's or strategist's mindset and roots the future in science, data, and math instead of dreaming or demanding. "Might" futurism deals with many possibilities, which need to be weighed against each other.

![Lunch Monk by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/lunch-monk.jpg)

Earlier in my career, I worked for a company that developed optimization software for ground handling at airports. 
This context does not allow you to plan exactly; you never know the exact order the planes will arrive. Sometimes a plane is delayed, sometimes they are too early, and then there is quite a lot going on before the plane can take off again. 
One lesson I took from this time is: "Do not decide until the last moment you need to." 
What it means is that you need to prepare for all scenarios and decide at the exact moment before the first scenario becomes invalid (and can no longer be used).
In the ground handling context, this could lead to baggage belts waiting on an already loaded plane because it's not decided yet which plane is the next one. But the moment the baggage belt takes longer to drive than the plane takes to arrive, the driver is sent off to the most likely position.

This kind of futurism is hard mental work. Getting the data, preparing the scenarios, and still missing many of them can be exhausting. But this is still the most practical way to prepare for the future.

## Don't

The fourth and final perspective Foster introduces is "don't" futurism. This is the domain of the professional pessimist, the risk manager, and the institutional contrarian. While the other perspectives focus on what can or might happen, people in this mindset are focused on a single, vital question: What could go wrong?

![Dangerous Bridge by Gemini](/img/posts/2026-10-25-book-review-csmd/dangerous-bridge.jpg)

This mindset stands in direct contrast to the bright-eyed "could" futurists we started with. If a "could" futurist stands on a stage and excitedly announces, "Let's put AI chips in everyone's brains for instant telepathy!", the "don't" futurist is the one immediately raising their hand to talk about brain-hacking, the permanent loss of cognitive privacy, and absolute ethical nightmares.

We see this perspective in action all the time in the tech world. Think of data privacy advocates fighting against surveillance capitalism, AI safety researchers pleading for development pauses, or regulators blocking risky, untested technologies from hitting the public market. They are the ones looking at the bridge in the picture above and pointing out that it's just too dangerous to cross, no matter how cool the destination on the other side might be.

Because of their cautious nature, "don't" futurists are frequently dismissed as stubborn roadblocks to innovation. However, in the EU, this kind of thinking is really helpful and used in many applications (e.g., German product safety laws, where you must prove first that a product causes no harm before selling it).
Foster also makes a compelling case for why we desperately need them. They provide the necessary friction to slow us down, ensuring that unchecked enthusiasm doesn't lead us into catastrophic and irreversible mistakes.

# Takeaway

These four perspectives help me a lot in my daily life. Of course, I cannot do a strategic foresight workshop to get a better idea of what I should cook for dinner, but I take different approaches on other topics in my life.
Using "could" futurism to generate visions allows me to shine a little bit of light into the future I want to happen. With "should" futurism, I can determine the environment and how it will influence the future. "Might" futurism helps me to analyze plans, actions, and strategies in detail and compare them in the different futures I expect. While "don't" futurism ensures I will not step over the line because I take warnings seriously and evaluate them against the benefits of my decisions.

I hope this post gave you a good summary of what's in the book. If that sounds like your kind of thing, you should definitely check it out.
